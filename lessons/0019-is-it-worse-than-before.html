  1. What does pass^k estimate, and why is it the column to trust for a skill you rely on?
    Show answer

    The chance that k trials in a row all pass: the pass rate multiplied by itself k times. A skill you rely on has to work every time, and pass^k describes that; pass@k only says whether one trial in k passed.

So far: You can count passing trials before and after an edit and put a range around each count, and you can price trials, run a model from your own provider, and fail a trial that costs too much.

This lesson answers: Can a check tell me that a change made things worse, in quality or in cost, and fail when it did?

Lesson 15 ended with a careful "cannot tell yet". On one model the judge's passes went from 3 of 9 to 8 of 9, and the ranges still touched. That is the honest answer to "did it get better?", but it is not the question you ask before every change you keep. The everyday question is narrower: is it any worse than before? A check that answers that, in quality and in tokens, and fails loudly when the answer is yes, is something you can run after every edit. Vally has a command for it. This lesson runs it on lesson 15's saved trials, so it spends judge calls and no agent runs.

Graders and side-by-side judging

Every grader so far looks at one trial at a time and holds it against a fixed check: a pattern, a script, a rubric. That is what you want for "does the entry cite the pull request?" It is less good at "is the new skill's entry better than the old one's?" A yes-or-no judge passes both entries, or fails both, and the difference between them disappears. Even lesson 14's 1-to-5 scale has to place each entry on its own, and a small improvement can land on the same number.

Pairwise judge

An LLM judge that is shown two answers to the same request, one from each version being compared, and says which is better and by how much. Vally's documentation calls this comparison mode.

Comparing two answers side by side is easier than scoring either one alone, for people and for models. "B leaves the coding detail out and A does not" is a judgement a model makes reliably even when both entries would get the same yes or no. So a pairwise judge notices smaller changes.

It has one well-known weakness. Shown two answers, judges tend to prefer one position, often the first, whatever is in it. The fix is to ask twice, with the two answers in opposite order, and to believe the verdict only when both orders agree. When they disagree, the honest result is a tie.

Use a pairwise judge to compare two versions of the same thing. It does not replace graders: it cannot tell you that both versions are wrong, only which is less wrong. Keep the graders for "is it right?" and use the side-by-side judgement for "is it worse?"

Sources: Vally docs, CLI: compare, on judging each pair twice with the positions swapped; Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge, 2023, section 3, on position bias in pairwise judges and swapping as the remedy.

What vally compare reports

vally compare runs a pairwise judge over two sets of saved trials and adds up the result. It starts no agent. The two sets have Vally's names: the baseline is the version you compare against, as in the baseline: line of an experiment file, and the treatment is the version you changed. It can be given them in two ways.

  • An experiment folder. vally compare <folder> compares every variant with the experiment's baseline. Lesson 15's baseline is haiku-before, so the two gpt-5.6-terra variants would be judged against claude-haiku-4.5, mixing a change of model into a question about the edit.
  • Two runs. --baseline and --treatment each name a run folder or a results.jsonl file. Each variant's folder inside the experiment folder holds its own results.jsonl, so this compares exactly two variants. The checkpoint uses this way, once per model.

Vally pairs the trials by stimulus name and trial number: the before skill's cache trial 0 with the after skill's cache trial 0, and so on. The judge reads each stimulus's rubric from the saved trials, the same rubric lesson 12 wrote, and gives each pair a verdict from the treatment's side.

VerdictScore
much better+1.0
slightly better+0.4
equal, or the two orders disagree0
slightly worse−0.4
much worse−1.0

Each pair costs two judge calls, one per order. The judge must be named with --judge-model: standalone compare does not read judge_model from the eval file or the saved trials, and without the option it uses Vally's default judge, which may be one of the models you are testing.     turns          -0.3 [-0.9, +0.2] ⚠ high variance
    tool calls     -0.3 [-0.9, +0.2] ⚠ high variance
    wall time (ms) -216 [-1217, +786] ⚠ high variance
    errors         +0.0 [+0.0, +0.0]
  1. The win rate is the share of pairs the treatment won: 6 of 9. Ties count against it, so it is not the same as "not worse".
  2. The mean of the nine scores, with a range. CI stands for confidence interval: the range lesson 15 described, here around a mean rather than a count, and the true mean is probably inside it. This range lies wholly above zero.
  3. A second test that ignores the judge. It looks only at the graders' pass or fail: pairs where only the treatment passed (+5) and pairs where only the baseline passed (−0). McNemar's test asks whether a split like 5 to 0 could easily be chance; p is that probability, and below 0.05 counts as significant. It is printed only when the saved trials were graded.
  4. After minus before for each measure, averaged over the pairs, each with a range. "High variance" means the pairs differed a lot from one another. Tokens are the total per trial from lesson 2.

Failing on a regression. Every command ends with an exit code, a number that scripts and automated checks read: 0 for success, anything else for failure. vally compare exits 1 whenever the judging did not finish: some pair could not be judged even after retries, with or without any option. A trial with no partner is different. It is left out before judging, a warning says so, and the exit code does not change; step 4 below shows one, and it exited 0. With --fail-on-regression the command also exits 1 when the treatment is significantly worse: its mean score is below zero and the whole range is below zero too. Nothing else counts. A rise in tokens, however clear, does not change the exit code, and neither does McNemar's test.

In a terminal, the shell keeps the last command's exit code in $?, and echo $? prints it. Run it straight after the command you want to check, because the next command replaces it.

A comparison prints "Mean score: −0.20 [95% CI −0.55, +0.15]". What does --fail-on-regression do?
Right. A negative mean is not enough: the whole range must lie below zero. This one reaches +0.15, so the trials cannot tell whether the change made things worse.
It exits 0. The option fails only when the mean is negative and the whole range lies below zero. This range reaches +0.15, so the trials cannot tell whether the change made things worse.

What it supports

Where it agrees with lesson 15, and where it goes further. For claude-haiku-4.5, lesson 15's counts, 3 of 9 and 8 of 9, gave ranges that just touched: cannot tell. McNemar's line says the same thing in another way. It looks only at pass or fail, finds five pairs that flipped from fail to pass and none the other way, and calls that not significant, because five coin tosses all landing the same way happen about one time in sixteen. The pairwise judge went further: a mean of +0.53 with its range wholly above zero. It could, because it compared each pair directly and gave credit for the pair that both versions failed but the after skill failed less badly. In our run, side-by-side judging found an improvement that counting passes could not yet show.

Hold that loosely, for three reasons. The nine pairs are three stimuli tried three times, not nine independent requests, so the range is narrower than the evidence really allows. The judge is a model, and you have not checked this pairwise judge against your own labels as lesson 13 did for the yes-or-no one. And it varies: judging the same pairs the other way round gave −0.38, not −0.53, because one pair went to the before skill that time. Run twice, that swapped comparison landed on both sides of the line: once at −0.49 with its range wholly below zero, which failed the check, and once at −0.38 with its range reaching +0.02, which passed it. Trust the sign and whether the range crosses zero, not the second decimal, and expect a range that ends near zero to land on either side of it on another run.

Worse in cost. On gpt-5.6-terra the judged quality did not move: 7 ties in 8, and a range from −0.07 to +0.17 that includes zero. The tokens did move. The after skill used about 11,000 more per trial, with a range from +665 to +21,599, wholly above zero; it took about one turn more. That is the "worse in cost" half of this lesson's question, and --fail-on-regression exited 0 through it, because the option gates on the judged score alone. To fail a run on cost, use the budget graders from lesson 18, set from the before trials. Read the metric lines every time, and use the exit code only for quality.

Every number in this section is from our runs on 2026-11-10.

The experiment's own comparison

vally experiment run also takes --compare, which runs this same judging over every variant against the baseline as soon as the experiment ends, using the judge named in the eval files. It reports and does not gate: it exits 1 only when the comparison cannot finish, and a regression it finds does not fail the command. Vally's own guide gives the order: run the experiment, then run vally compare on its folder with --fail-on-regression as the step that can fail. We have not run --compare, because it needs a fresh experiment with agent runs.

Sources: Vally docs, Run Your First Experiment, on --compare reporting and vally compare --fail-on-regression gating; CLI: experiment, on the judge settings --compare uses.

Vally docs, CLI: compare

The whole command on one page: the two ways to name the versions, how pairs are judged, the verdict scale, what is printed and what each exit code means. Read it to the end; the last part, on partial comparisons, says what happens when some pairs cannot be judged.

Exercises

  1. From memory: what must be true for --fail-on-regression to exit 1, and name two things in the printed block it ignores.
    Show model answer

    The treatment's mean score is negative and its whole 95% range lies below zero. (With or without the option, the command also exits 1 when some pair could not be judged; a trial with no partner is only left out and does not change the exit code.) It ignores the metric deltas, such as tokens and wall time, and McNemar's test.

  2. In our run, the edit made gpt-5.6-terra use about 11,000 more tokens per trial, and the regression check passed. You want the next edit to fail if it does that again. What would you add, and from which numbers would you set it?
    Show model answer

    A token-budget grader, from lesson 18, on these stimuli, with its max set just above the tokens of the trials you accept now (the before trials used about 49,000 each in our run). Then a trial of an edit that costs more fails like any other grader's failure. Test the budget on the saved trials with vally grade first, so you know it does not fail good work.

  3. Why does the pairwise judge read each pair twice, and what does a tie mean in the comparison record?
    Show model answer

    Judges tend to favour one position. Reading the pair in both orders and keeping only a verdict both orders agree on stops that from deciding the result. A tie means the judge found the two equal, or that the two orders disagreed, which the record's reason says ("Position-swap inconsistent … Defaulting to tie").

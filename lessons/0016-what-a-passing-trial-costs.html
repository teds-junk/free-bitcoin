This lesson answers: Which model, at which reasoning effort, gives the most passing trials for what it costs?

Lesson 15's table ended with a row of tokens, and it is tempting to read the variant with fewer tokens as the cheaper one. It is not that simple. This lesson's run compares gpt-5.6-terra with claude-haiku-5.5, a newer model in the same family as the course's claude-haiku-4.5; the section on reasoning effort says why we switched. In our run a terra trial used a median of 61.0k tokens against Haiku's 105.1k, and yet each passing terra trial cost about thirteen times as much: each model has its own price per token. And a cheap trial that fails has bought you nothing. This lesson works out what you pay for each trial that passes, for two models at two settings, and picks one.

Cost per passing trial

Cost per passing trial

A variant's total cost over all its trials, divided by the number of trials that passed. It is what one useful result costs you.

Dividing by passing trials, not all trials, is the point. Suppose variant A costs 2 AIU a trial and passes 5 of 10, and variant B costs 3 AIU a trial and passes 10 of 10. A spends 20 AIU for 5 passes, 4 AIU each. B spends 30 AIU for 10 passes, 3 AIU each. The cheaper trial is the dearer result. Every failure is paid for and then thrown away, and someone still has to do the work.

With several variants, put each one's pass rate beside its cost and look for any variant that another beats on both counts: one that costs more and passes no more often. A variant beaten on both counts can be dropped. Those left are real choices, a trade between paying more and passing more, and which to pick depends on what a failure costs you.

This way of comparing comes from research on agent benchmarks. Kapoor and colleagues found agent leaderboards that ranked on accuracy alone, where simple agents that retried a few times matched the leaders at a fraction of the cost. They argued that every evaluation should report cost beside accuracy.

One thing the cost you will see leaves out: judge calls. A trial graded by an LLM judge costs the agent's run plus the judge's, and Vally records only the first. For an eval with a judge, the true cost is higher than the figures in this lesson's calculator.

Variant A costs 1 AIU a trial and passes 3 of 10. Variant B costs 2 AIU a trial and passes 9 of 10. Which costs less per passing trial?
Right. A spends 10 AIU for 3 passes, about 3.3 each. B spends 20 AIU for 9 passes, about 2.2 each. A's cheaper trials buy fewer results.
B. A spends 10 AIU for 3 passes, about 3.3 AIU each; B spends 20 AIU for 9 passes, about 2.2 each. Cost per passing trial divides by passes, so a cheap trial that fails makes the result dearer.
Source: Kapoor et al., AI Agents That Matter, 2024-07, a preprint. Their term for this kind of comparison is cost-controlled evaluation; "cost per passing trial" is our plain description of the figure this lesson computes.

Vally prints no total cost for a variant. The experiment's table gives total tokens, its report.md gives median tokens per stimulus, and neither turns tokens into cost. The course's calculator does that. It reads the results.jsonl files you pick, in your browser, and for each variant adds up the cost of every trial, counts the trials that passed and the ones that ended in ERROR, and divides. It tells you when a variant's trials have no cost recorded, and it converts AIU to dollars at $0.01 each, the rate from lesson 2.

Reasoning effort as a variant

Some models can work through a problem in hidden reasoning before they reply or call a tool. reasoning_effort sets how much of that the agent's model does. More effort usually means more tokens and more time on each call, and sometimes better results. It goes under defaults in an eval file, beside model, so an experiment can vary it exactly as it varies the model.

The values a model accepts depend on the model. Vally checks the value against your account's list of models before any session starts, so asking for a value no model has is a free way to find out. In our account:

ModelReasoning efforts it accepts
claude-haiku-4.5none: "does not support reasoning effort configuration"
claude-haiku-5.5low, medium, high, xhigh, max
gpt-5.6-terranone, low, medium, high, xhigh, max

The course's first model, claude-haiku-4.5, refuses any effort. So for this lesson the two Haiku variants use its newer sibling, claude-haiku-5.5, which takes one. This is the experiment file:

evals/cost.experiment.yaml

  - changelog.eval.yaml1

vary: [/defaults/model, /defaults/reasoning_effort]2

  runs: 33

  haiku-low:
    overrides:
      model: claude-haiku-5.5
      reasoning_effort: low4
  haiku-high:
    overrides:
      model: claude-haiku-5.5
      reasoning_effort: high
  terra-low:
    overrides:
      model: gpt-5.6-terra
      reasoning_effort: low
  terra-high:
    overrides:
      model: gpt-5.6-terra
      reasoning_effort: high
  1. The five stimuli from lessons 5 and 6 that this eval file holds, with their code graders. No judge, so the costs below are the whole cost.
  2. The variants may differ in model and in reasoning effort, and in nothing else. All of them use the skill as it stands.
  3. Three trials of each stimulus: fifteen trials per variant, sixty in all.
  4. The reasoning effort for this variant. low and high are values both models accept.
Sources: Vally docs, Eval Spec reference, defaults.reasoning_effort; the accepted values are from our account on 2026-11-10 and will differ on yours.

What the table says

In our run every trial passed, so cost per passing trial was simply cost per trial, and the differences were all in cost. Three of the four variants are beaten on both counts by haiku-low. haiku-high passed no more often and cost more: about 14% more per trial, with about a third more output tokens (a median of 876 against 661) and the slower Haiku trials. Both terra variants passed no more often and cost about thirteen times as much. On terra, high effort changed almost nothing: 2.76 AIU per passing trial against 2.74.

Notice what the token counts would have told you on their own. terra used the fewest tokens, a median of 49,000 to 61,000 a trial against Haiku's 105,000 to 106,000, and cost the most by far. On our account a Haiku 5.5 trial was recorded at 0.14 to 0.39 AIU and a terra trial at 1.59 to 5.20 AIU. Tokens measure work, not price.

Price per token differs by model

The price of a token depends on the model, even between two versions of one family. Lesson 2's claude-haiku-4.5 trial cost 2.89 AIU for 74,052 tokens, about 3.9 AIU per 100,000 tokens. The claude-haiku-5.5 record in this lesson cost 0.23 AIU for 105,041 tokens, about 0.22 AIU per 100,000. On our account the newer Haiku was about 18 times cheaper per token than the older one.

That is why this lesson measures cost per passing trial for each model, rather than carrying one model's figure over to another. It is also why lesson 2's "a typical trial costs about 2 cents" holds only for the models used there, claude-haiku-4.5 and gpt-5.6-terra. A haiku-low trial here cost about a fifth of a cent. Change the model and you have to measure again.

Now apply lesson 15's habit. Fifteen passes in fifteen trials gives a range of 80% to 100%, and every variant had that. The four ranges are the same, so three trials per stimulus cannot tell the variants apart on pass rate. They do not need to here. The case for haiku-low rests on cost. Each model's trials cost different amounts, within a band about three times wide: 0.14 to 0.39 AIU for Haiku 5.5, 1.59 to 5.20 AIU for terra. But the two bands do not overlap: every Haiku trial cost less than the cheapest terra trial. The fair statement is "as good as far as these trials can tell, and far cheaper". It is also a statement about these five requests only. They are the stimuli the skill now passes easily; lesson 12's wording requests, graded by a judge, are where the models differed, and they should be priced the same way before you change the model for everything.

Prices change, and your account's may differ from ours. Treat these numbers as a method you can repeat, not as facts about the models.

What it comes to in dollars

At $0.01 per AIU, and using averages from our runs:

  • This experiment: 89.21 AIU for 60 trials, about 89 cents.
  • A quick check after every edit: lesson 21 runs these five stimuli on two models, ten trials. Across our clean re-run of lessons 1 to 15, the 213 trials with usage recorded, nearly all on claude-haiku-4.5 and gpt-5.6-terra, averaged about 2.2 AIU, so that is about 22 AIU, 22 cents, an edit. Twenty edits in a month is about $4.40. The same five on haiku-low alone would be about 1 AIU, a cent an edit.
  • Every stimulus every night: lesson 21's full run is thirty trials, about 66 AIU at the same average, or about $20 over thirty nights. Its judged stimuli add judge calls, which Vally does not count.
  • What verifying this course cost, for scale: the pilot before the plan, 42 trials, about 70 AIU. Writing lessons 1 to 15 took 251 agent runs and 66 judge calls; we did not total their cost, and at the 2.2 AIU average the runs come to about 550 AIU. Our clean re-run of lessons 1 to 15 took 249 agent runs, with usage recorded for 213 of them, about 473 AIU, and 52 judge calls on top. In all, about $11 of agent runs, plus the judge calls.

None of that is large for one person. It grows with stimuli, trials, models and how often you run, which is why the cheapest check that answers your question comes first, and why lesson 21's check after every edit stays small. Your plan's monthly allowance is spent first, as lesson 2 said, so much of this may cost you nothing extra.

    Show model answer

    A variant's total cost divided by the number of its trials that passed. Dividing by passes charges each useful result with the cost of the failures it took to get it, so a cheap variant that fails often can be the dearer one.

  2. One variant passes 12 of 15 trials at 0.3 AIU a trial. Another passes 15 of 15 at 2.7 AIU. Is either beaten on both counts? What would you need to know to choose?
    Show model answer

    Neither. The first costs 4.5 AIU for 12 passes, about 0.38 per passing trial; the second 40.5 AIU for 15, 2.7 each. The second may pass more often, but the ranges, 55% to 93% and 80% to 100%, overlap, so these trials cannot say. To choose you need more trials, to know whether the pass rates really differ, and what a failure costs you: if each failure means a person fixing a changelog by hand, a few more passes may be worth seven times the price.

    Show model answer

    Their trials' calls did not go through Copilot, so Vally recorded tokens and no cost. They multiply the tokens by their service's price per token themselves. Lesson 17 does this.

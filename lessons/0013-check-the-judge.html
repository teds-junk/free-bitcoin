So far: You have a judge that says yes or no to "written for people who use the tool", and your own yes or no for the same twelve entries, written before the judge ran.

This lesson answers: Can I trust the judge's verdicts?

You tested your code graders two ways: against a known-correct answer and against known-bad ones. A judge deserves the same suspicion and cannot be tested the same way, because there is no golden patch for "reads well to a user". What you have in its place is your own judgement, written down in lesson 12 before the judge spoke. Putting the two lists side by side is the whole method. The only subtlety is how to count.

A judge is measured, not trusted

Like any grader, a judge can be wrong in two directions. It can say yes to an entry you would reject: a failure slips through, and you believe the skill is better than it is. Or it can say no to an entry you would accept: a false alarm, and you go hunting for a problem that is not there.

The obvious measure is agreement: on what share of the trials did the judge and I say the same thing? It is a poor measure, because it hides which direction the errors run in. Suppose you label twenty entries, and eighteen are fine. A judge that says yes to everything agrees with you eighteen times out of twenty, which is 90%, and it has caught neither of the two bad entries. It is useless, and its agreement looks excellent.

So count the two directions separately. Of the entries you said yes to, how many did the judge pass? Of the entries you said no to, how many did it fail? Two numbers, and a judge has to do well on both.

How well is well enough, and on how many labels? The practitioners this course has leaned on suggest aiming above 90% on each number, and labelling a hundred or more examples for a judge you intend to depend on. Twelve labels, which is what you have, can show that a judge is badly wrong. They cannot show that it is reliably right.

Three things undo this measurement, and each means labelling again: changing the rubric's wording, changing the judge model, and pointing the judge at a different kind of request. When you reword a rubric to fix a disagreement, keep some labelled entries aside that you do not look at while rewording. Otherwise you are fitting the rubric to the examples you are testing it on.

Sources: Husain and Shankar, validate-evaluator skill, for the two rates and the 90% aim; AI Evals FAQ, reissued 2026-09-18, for the number of labels.

TPR and TNR

The two numbers have standard names.

TPR, the true positive rate

Of the trials you labelled yes, the share the judge also passed. "Positive" is the statistician's word for yes.

TNR, the true negative rate

Of the trials you labelled no, the share the judge also failed. "Negative" is the word for no.

To work them out, sort every trial into one of four boxes, by what the judge said and what you said.

An invented judge, to show the arithmetic

Suppose you labelled twenty entries: twelve yes and eight no. The judge passed eleven of your twelve, and also passed three of your eight.

TPR is 11 out of 12, which is 92%. TNR is 5 out of 8, which is 63%. Agreement is 16 out of 20, which is 80%, and sounds respectable. The TNR tells the real story: this judge lets through more than a third of the entries you would reject.

Read down the columns, not across the rows. Each rate uses one column: TPR the left, TNR the right. The calculator below does the division. It starts with the invented numbers above; change any box and the rates update.

A low TPR means false alarms: the judge is stricter than you. A low TNR means missed failures: it is more lenient than you. Which matters more depends on what you use the judge for, but a judge with either number low is not measuring your criterion.

Count, compare, decide

You will count your twelve trials into the four boxes, work out the two rates, look closely at any disagreement, and write down what you will and will not rely on the judge for. Nothing here runs an agent or a judge.

What twelve labels can show

Our judge agreed with every label, and it is worth being exact about what that shows. It shows that on these twelve entries, this judge model applied this criterion the way we do. With six "no" labels, a single miss would have put the TNR at 83%. The sample is too small to tell a judge that is right 99% of the time from one that is right 85% of the time.

It is still a real result. A judge that had passed everything, or had failed entries for reasons unrelated to the criterion, would have shown up at once, and that is the most common way judges go wrong.

Exercises

    Show model answer

    TPR is the share of your yes labels the judge also passed; a low TPR means false alarms, a judge stricter than you. TNR is the share of your no labels the judge also failed; a low TNR means missed failures, a judge more lenient than you.

  2. A teammate reports that their judge "agrees with human labels 94% of the time". Write the two questions you would ask.
    Show model answer

    "What are the TPR and the TNR separately?" and "How many of the labels were no?" If nearly all the labels were yes, 94% agreement is what a judge that always says yes would score.
